As the Garden Goes to Sleep, the Studio Is Wide Awake

Autumn at Garden Shed Clay — putting the garden to bed, preparing for winter, and making pottery for the holidays.

October 27, 2026 · Lester Morris

There is a particular feeling to the garden in autumn. The mornings are cooler, the air is fresh, and the sunlight has changed. The plants that once seemed determined to take over every available inch of space just a few weeks ago have slowed or stopped completely.

Here in Philadelphia, the garden is gradually making its transition from the hot and humid exuberance of summer to the quieter, resigned rhythms of fall and winter.

Some plants have finished their work for the year. Others are still offering a colorful last show before the first hard frost. Perennials are beginning their long winter rest, storing energy below the soil for another growing season. The garden beds have been cleared, mulched, and transitioned to fall. I have planted collard greens, cauliflower, and lettuce. Garlic and shallots are planted and will lie relatively dormant until spring. Plans for spring are underway, and seed catalogs will begin arriving in the mail very soon.

I've always enjoyed this part of the gardening year. There is something satisfying about looking back at what worked, what didn't, and what I might do differently next time. A garden is never really finished. It simply moves from one season to another.

A Different Kind of Growing Season

While the garden is winding down, something quite different is happening inside the pottery studio.

The studio has moved into “holiday mode.”

Mugs, bowls, plates, and other everyday pieces are taking shape with the coming holidays in mind. It's the time of year when the shelves begin filling with work in various stages of completion: freshly thrown pieces drying, pots waiting to be trimmed, bisque-fired work ready for glazing, and finished pieces emerging from the kiln. The kiln is HUMMING!

There is a rhythm to pottery that feels surprisingly similar to gardening.

Both require centering and focus. Both involve a fair amount of planning. And both have a way of reminding you that, despite your best intentions, you are not entirely in control of the outcome. Sometimes clay finds its own way and lets you know you aren’t its boss. “Die Schussel ist von Ert gemacht / Wann sie verbricht der Haefner lacht” (The dish is made of earth; when it breaks, the potter laughs).

A seed doesn't always germinate. A glaze doesn't always behave as expected. Clay cracks during the firing process. Sometimes the results are disappointing, and sometimes they're better than anything you imagined.

That's part of what makes both pursuits so rewarding.

Making Things for Everyday Life

As the holidays approach, I find myself thinking about the kinds of pottery people use most often.

A favorite mug that becomes part of a morning ritual. A bowl brought out for a family meal. A plate that gets used so frequently that nobody thinks of it as something special anymore.

Those are the pieces I particularly enjoy making.

Handmade pottery doesn't have to be reserved for special occasions. In fact, I think some of its greatest pleasure comes from being used every day.

Each piece carries small reminders of how it was made: the marks of the potter's hands, slight variations in shape, the way a glaze breaks across an edge or settles into a curve.

No two pieces are exactly alike, and that's very much the point.

Preparing for the Holidays

The weeks ahead will be busy ones in the studio.

There will be more making, trimming, glazing, and firing as I prepare mugs, bowls, plates, and other pieces for the holiday season. Some will become gifts, others will find their way into kitchens and homes, and a few may stay right here in the studio.

I'll be sharing some of that work as it progresses, including new pieces, kiln results, and the occasional experiment that doesn't turn out quite as planned. New pots will be appearing on my website more frequently.

Meanwhile, outside the studio door, the garden will continue settling into its winter rest.

And before long, the days will begin growing longer again, and the garden will awaken once more.

For now, though, it's time to enjoy the change of seasons, appreciate what the garden has given us, and get back to making pottery.

From the garden and the studio, thank you for following along.
